Quitting vaping sounds simple enough: just stop picking up the vape.

If only it were that easy.

When you've been vaping for months or years, it stops being just a nicotine habit. Your
brain quietly wires vaping into almost everything you do. Driving, eating, working,
relaxing, scrolling, coffee, friends, or being bored for approximately 14 seconds.

That's why quitting feels so hard. You're not giving up one thing. You're breaking a whole
collection of routines that snuck into your day while you weren't looking.

The good news? The cycle breaks. There's no single magic method: some people stop cold
turkey, some taper down, some use nicotine replacement like patches or gum. What matters
is having a plan for both halves of the problem, the nicotine and the habit.

Here are ten simple steps that do exactly that.

## 1. Work out how much nicotine you're actually using

Before you quit, put a number on the thing you're quitting.

Check the strength on your e-liquid or device, usually in mg/mL, then look at how much
liquid you go through and how fast. Say your liquid is 20 mg/mL and a 20 mL bottle lasts
a week:

20 mg × 20 mL = 400 mg of nicotine in the bottle

400 mg ÷ 7 days ≈ 57 mg a day, in the liquid you vaped

That's the nicotine in the liquid, not what your body absorbed, and vaping doesn't
deliver nicotine the way cigarettes do. So ignore anyone claiming your vape "equals"
some exact number of cigarettes.

The math is still worth doing, because it's usually eye-opening. So is counting your
puffs for a few days: most people [guess their count low, often by half](/blog/how-many-puffs-a-day-is-a-lot).
Understanding the habit is step one of changing it.

## 2. Pick your strategy, then stop negotiating

Two main roads. Stop completely, or lower your nicotine gradually until you reach zero.

Cold turkey means taking the hit head-on. Rough for a few days, but some people want
exactly that: a clean line, a before and an after. Tapering means stepping down in
strength so the drop never arrives all at once. And nicotine replacement, patches, gum,
lozenges, is a conversation worth having with a pharmacist or doctor.

There is no prize for choosing the most miserable option.

There is, however, a familiar trap:

"I'll quit Monday."

"Okay, next Monday."

"Well, January is basically next year."

At some point the decision has to become real. Pick the date. Tell someone. Close the
negotiation.

## 3. Make your home a vape free zone

Vaping is automatic. If the vape is sitting beside you, your brain doesn't have to hold
a committee meeting before reaching for it. The hand just goes.

So remove the easy access. Device, pods, e-liquid, chargers, the emergency one in the
glovebox. All of it. Home and car, vape free.

When starting again means getting dressed, finding a shop and buying a new device, you've
built a gap between the urge and the act. That gap is where a different choice fits.

## 4. Give your mouth and hands something to do

One of the hardest parts of quitting is weirdly physical. You'll catch your hand drifting
to a pocket that has nothing in it. Your mouth wants a job and won't say which one.

Stock up before day one:

- Sugar free gum
- Mints
- Sunflower seeds
- Crunchy vegetables
- Toothpicks
- Cold water through a straw
- A stress ball or fidget toy

None of this replaces vaping, and it doesn't have to. The job is to interrupt the
automatic reach and inhale. Chewing gum on your commute sounds trivial. It's also how
"car" stops meaning "vape."

## 5. Expect the first few days to be rough

If you quit and suddenly feel irritable, restless, foggy and obsessed with vaping, don't
conclude that quitting isn't working. That's what working feels like. The peak usually
lands on [day two or three](/blog/how-long-does-vaping-withdrawal-last), then eases.

Here's the most useful number in this whole article: an individual craving runs about
**15 to 20 minutes**.

You'll think, "I absolutely need my vape right now."

Twenty minutes later: "Okay. I'm actually fine."

That swing is the entire lesson. A craving feels enormous from the inside and it does not
stay at maximum forever. So shrink the assignment. Not "I can never vape again," which is
a terrifying sentence on a bad Tuesday. Just "I can get through the next twenty minutes."
Drink water. Walk. Text someone. Breathe. Play something dumb on your phone.

You don't have to defeat every craving for the rest of your life today. Just this one.

## 6. Make your distraction list before you need it

Deciding what to do during a craving is like grocery shopping while starving. Decide in
advance instead.

Write down 10 to 20 things you can start within a minute: a short walk, a shower, making
tea, cold water, texting a friend, a quick game, cleaning something, music, stretching, a
few slow breaths, or simply leaving the room where the craving started.

It sounds too simple. It works because vaping runs on autopilot, and a list creates a
pause between "I want to vape" and "the vape is in my hand." Everything good about
quitting lives inside that pause.

## 7. Find your triggers

You don't vape at random. You vape first thing in the morning, with coffee, after meals,
in the car, on breaks, when stressed, when bored, when drinking, when someone else pulls
theirs out. These are learned associations, and they can be unlearned.

Change the choreography, don't just endure it. If you always vape after lunch, don't sit
in your usual spot, get up and walk. If you vape while driving, the gum lives in the car
now. If certain nights out are basically vaping with a social layer on top, it's fine to
skip a few of them early on.

Each time, you're teaching your brain a small update: we do this part of the day without
vaping now. Eventually the new version is just how the day goes.

## 8. Write down what you're feeling

Cravings are dramatic narrators. In the moment, "I want to vape" presents itself as "I
cannot function without vaping." Paper catches the exaggeration.

When a craving hits, write one line. "I'm irritated." "I'm bored." "I can't focus." Then
check back 30 minutes later. Almost every time, the feeling has moved, and seeing that in
your own handwriting teaches you what no article can: cravings rise and fall, they don't
just pile up.

Three questions are enough. What triggered me? What did I do instead? Did it pass? After
a week you own an instruction manual for quitting, written by the one person your
triggers actually apply to.

## 9. Change the story you tell about yourself

Easy to skip. Quietly powerful.

"I'm trying to quit" keeps a door open. It makes vaping the default you're straining
against. "I don't vape" is a different kind of sentence. Not a struggle, just a fact
about you that cravings occasionally argue with.

It also changes what a slip means. If you're "trying to quit," one puff proves the trying
failed. If you're someone who doesn't vape, a slip is a data point: something happened,
figure out what, adjust, carry on. Shaming yourself into quitting doesn't work. It mostly
makes people vape about how bad they feel for vaping.

## 10. Don't do it alone

Tell someone you trust that you're quitting, and ask them to check in during the first
week. One text on day three matters more than it sounds.

Stacking help is also what the evidence backs. In a 2025 JAMA trial of young near-daily
vapers, varenicline plus counselling plus text support produced a **51% quit rate at 12
weeks**, against 6% for text support alone. Varenicline is an old, cheap prescription
pill, worth a conversation with a doctor. Free text programmes with real evidence exist
too: **This is Quitting** from Truth Initiative, and **quitSTART** from the National
Cancer Institute.

Sometimes the smartest move isn't trying harder. It's getting more help.

## What about cold turkey?

If that's your road, go in with your eyes open. The first several days can be genuinely
rough: irritability, anxiety, strong cravings, worst around day two or three. For most
people it eases noticeably within the first week. Your timeline may differ, and comparing
it to a stranger's is a losing game.

The goal isn't to prove you can suffer the most. The goal is to quit.

## The bottom line: make vaping harder and better choices easier

The easiest way to quit vaping isn't one secret trick. It's several small changes pointed
in the same direction. Remove the vape. Know your numbers. Pick a strategy. Prep your
substitutes. Rewire the routines. Expect the cravings instead of being ambushed by them.
Get backup.

Most importantly: a craving is a suggestion, not a command.

Your brain says, "We need a vape."

You get to answer, "Nice suggestion. We're going for a walk instead."

At first, you'll have that conversation a lot. Then less. Then one day you'll notice you
drove somewhere without thinking about vaping. Finished a meal without reaching for it.
Survived a stressful afternoon without needing a hit.

That's when quitting stops feeling like a fight and starts feeling like your normal life
again. You don't need to feel perfectly ready, and you don't need unlimited willpower.
You just need to get through the next craving, then the next one, and keep stacking days
that don't include vaping.
