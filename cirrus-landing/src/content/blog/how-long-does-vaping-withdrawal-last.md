Most people brace for a terrible first week and get ambushed on day two. Knowing roughly
when the hard part lands makes it much easier to sit through, so here's the shape of it.

## The timeline

| When | What's usually happening |
|---|---|
| 4 to 24 hours | First symptoms. Irritability, a short fuse, hunting for the device without deciding to |
| Day 2 to 3 | The peak. Worst concentration, worst mood, strongest cravings |
| Day 4 to 10 | Steady improvement. Most physical symptoms settle in this window |
| Week 2 to 4 | Mostly through it. Sleep and appetite normalise |
| Beyond | Occasional cravings, usually cue-driven, weaker and further apart each time |

The peak arriving on day two or three is the part worth internalising. People who expect
the worst on day five read a bad Tuesday as evidence that quitting isn't working for them,
when it's the most predictable moment in the whole process.

## What it feels like

Irritability, poor concentration, restlessness, low mood, more appetite than usual, and
sleep that goes strange for a bit. Plus cravings, which are their own thing.

None of that means something is wrong with you. It means a receptor system that had adapted
to regular nicotine is readjusting to none, and that readjustment has a duration.

> Individual cravings last 15 to 20 minutes.
>
> <cite>Nicotine craving literature</cite>

That figure is worth carrying around. A craving feels open-ended while you're inside it, and
it isn't. Twenty minutes of doing something with your hands is usually the entire ask.

## Why tapering changes the shape

Cold turkey takes the whole drop at once. That's why the first 72 hours are so sharp, and
it's why a method with a genuinely good success rate for some people fails most.

Tapering doesn't remove withdrawal. It spreads it. Each step down is small enough that your
body adjusts before the next one, so you never meet the full peak at once. You're trading
speed for the odds of it holding.

That trade is the entire reason [Cirrus counts your real puffs and paces a taper](/) instead
of asking you to stop dead on a Monday. If you've never counted, start there: most people
are out by half, and [a taper needs a real starting number](/blog/how-many-puffs-a-day-is-a-lot)
rather than an estimate.

## What actually helps

The strongest evidence in vaping cessation right now is for combining things rather than
picking one. In a 2025 JAMA trial of 261 near-daily vapers aged 16 to 25, varenicline plus
counselling plus text support produced a **51% quit rate at 12 weeks**, against 6% for text
support alone.

Varenicline is an old, cheap, generic prescription pill, and it's worth a conversation with
a doctor. The Cochrane review current to July 2025 rates that evidence promising but
low-certainty, and finds text-message programmes helpful for young adults. We'll say that
plainly rather than oversell it.

Free options exist too. **This is Quitting** from Truth Initiative is a text programme with
good evidence behind it, and **quitSTART** from the National Cancer Institute costs nothing.

## One thing to expect that nobody mentions

Around week two, when the physical part has eased, a lot of people get careless. The hard
bit appears to be over, one puff seems survivable, and the count creeps back.

That's not withdrawal. That's habit, and it outlasts the chemistry by a wide margin. The
plan you follow in week three matters more than the one you followed in week one. If you
don't have one yet, [here's the easiest way to quit vaping, in ten simple steps](/blog/easiest-way-to-quit-vaping),
and it covers the habit half too.
